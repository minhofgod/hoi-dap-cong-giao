## The single most important correction in either audit

**Trent, Session VI canon 9 — the ellipsis inverts the canon.** The entry quotes:

> "…by faith alone the impious is justified … let him be anathema."

The canon actually reads (verified at the Tanner text): "If any one saith, that by faith alone the
impious is justified; **in such wise as to mean, that nothing else is required to co-operate in order
to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary, that he be
prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will**; let him be anathema."

The deleted clause is the whole meaning. As printed, the site has Trent anathematizing *sola fide*
flat — **the Protestant-polemic caricature of Trent, published on a Catholic apologetics site as
Trent's own words.** And the page contradicts itself: the entry's own `sections[justification]` and
`apologetics` both correctly explain that Trent rejected the slogan only when taken to exclude
cooperation. The prose gets it right and the pull-quote destroys it — and the pull-quote is what a
reader screenshots.

One ellipsis, in the one place the site most needs precision, on the doctrine that defines the
division the site exists to address. **Fix this first, regardless of what else is triaged.**

## Structural findings (all 21 councils)


## C1. The `quote` field does not hold what it claims to hold

The single systemic finding of this audit so far, and the councils' analogue of the Fathers' quote
problem — but worse in kind, because a council entry's `quote` is presented as **an ecumenical
council speaking**, not as one Father's opinion.

**Three independent verifiers have now found the same defect**, on the first three councils to
report:

| # | Council | What the entry presents as the council's words | What the council actually says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | Lateran I | "No one may buy or sell sacred offices or the spiritual gifts of God" — attributed to "First Lateran Council, 1123 (against simony)" | **Appears in no canon.** Canon 1 (Tanner): "we altogether forbid anyone **to be ordained or promoted in the church of God for money**." Confirmed independently by the coordinating session |
| 6 | Constantinople III | "…not opposed but **cooperating with each other**" — attributed to "The Third Council of Constantinople, 681" | This is **CCC 475's gloss**, not the council. The definition is deliberately **asymmetric**: the human will is "following, and not resisting or struggling, rather in fact **subject to** his divine and all powerful will." The entry softens the council on the exact point the council existed to settle |
| 2 | Constantinople I | `sections[creed]` has the council calling the Spirit "**là Thiên Chúa**" (is God) | The 381 creed pointedly says τὸ κύριον, "**the Lord**", and applies neither *θεός* nor *homoousios* to the Spirit — Gregory of Nazianzus broke with the council over that omission. The entry's **own English says "the Lord"**, so vi and en contradict each other on the page |
| 3 | Ephesus | "Mary is truly the Mother of God, for the one she conceived in her womb is the Word made flesh" — attributed flatly to "The Council of Ephesus, 431" | **In no decree, canon or session act of 431.** It compresses **Cyril's first anathema** — and the Twelve Anathemas were only *read into* the Ephesine proceedings, not promulgated as a conciliar definition; they were formally endorsed only at Constantinople II in 553 |

**Ephesus was the test case named in the brief, and it failed as predicted.** By contrast the
**Chalcedon** quote is a genuine extract from the Definition, correctly attributed — recorded as
`confirmed` so a later pass does not "fix" it. So the defect is real but not universal: each quote
has to be located individually.

### The Constance variant — quoting the editor, not the council

Constance is the sharpest case and a **distinct failure mode**, worth naming because it changes what
a fixer should look for. Its `quote` — "There had been three claiming to be pope… now there is one
alone, Martin V, recognized by the whole Church", sourced as *"On the election of Pope Martin V,
1417"* — is not composed by the site at all. It paraphrases **Tanner's own modern editorial
introduction**: "When the council of Constance opened, Christians owed obedience to three different
popes… Martin V was elected pope on 11 November 1417 and he was regarded as the legitimate pontiff
by the church as a whole."

Confirmed independently by the coordinating session: papalencyclicals.net labels that block "the
introduction given by Tanner in *Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils*". **A twentieth-century editor's
headnote is on the site as an ecumenical council speaking.** No decree of 1417 bears the cited title;
the only act on the election is session 41's *Decretum de electoribus Romani Pontificis*, which
regulates voters.

The lesson for pass 2: **finding the sentence on a page of conciliar texts is not enough.** Whoever
sourced these was reading the right page and took the wrong part of it. Every quote needs locating
inside a numbered session, canon or constitution — not merely somewhere in the document.

|---|---|---|
| Chalcedon, Lateran IV, Lyon I, Lyon II, Florence, Vatican I, Vatican II | Vienne, Constantinople I (`quote` block is fine; the error is in `sections[creed]`) | **Ephesus, Lateran I, Constantinople III, Constance** |

### Mechanical risk survey — all 21

`quote.source` attributes the quotation two different ways:

- **5 of 21 name no document at all**, only the council and year: **Ephesus, Constantinople II,
  Constantinople III, Nicaea II, Lyon II.** A quotation attributed to a council with no decree,
  canon or session named cannot be checked by a reader, and is exactly where a composed summary can
  sit unnoticed. Constantinople III is already confirmed defective; Ephesus is under verification.
- **16 name a document** (e.g. "Pastor Aeternus, 1870", "Decree on Justification, Session VI").

**The survey is a risk indicator, not a diagnostic.** Lateran I's source *does* name the council and
its subject — and its quote is still composed. So the 16 "named" entries are not thereby safe; each
still needs its text located. What the survey does show is where to look first.

### A separate inconsistency in the same field

**2 of 21 put a Bible verse in the `quote` field**: Constantinople IV cites **John 17:21** and
Lateran II cites **Ephesians 4:5**. Not wrong in itself — a scripture verse can head a council page —
but it means the field carries three different kinds of content across the section (conciliar text,
scripture, composed summary), and a reader has no way to tell which they are looking at. Worth an
editorial decision rather than a per-entry fix.

## C1b. The same problem reaches `documents[]` and `documents_note`

Ephesus shows the defect is not confined to the `quote` field:

- **`documents[0]` names a document that does not exist** — "The definition of Mary as Mother of
  God". Ephesus issued no dogmatic definition on Mary. Its session VI decree did close to the
  opposite: it reaffirmed Nicaea and **forbade the composition of any new creed**.
- **`documents_note` claims the prayer "Holy Mary, Mother of God" comes from Ephesus.** Confirmed
  independently by the coordinating session: the ending *Sancta Maria, Mater Dei, ora pro nobis
  peccatoribus* is a **15th-century** addition to the Ave Maria, found in St Bernardine of Siena
  (1380–1444) and the Carthusians — roughly a **thousand years** after the council.

Both are the same failure as C1 in a different field: a true general claim (Ephesus vindicated
*Theotokos*; the title entered popular devotion) hardened into a specific documentary claim that the
record does not support. Any verifier reading only `quote` would have missed both.

## C3. `ccc_refs` cites paragraphs *about the topic*, not paragraphs *about the council*

Unlike Giáo Phụ, where all 30 entries had `ccc_refs: []`, **17 of 21 councils cite Catechism
paragraphs** — 34 citations in total. Spec item 6 is therefore a real check here, and it is already
failing in a consistent way.

| Council | Cites | What that paragraph actually is |
|---|---|---|
| 5 Constantinople II | CCC **466** | About **Nestorius**, and it cites **Ephesus 431** — not this council |
| 12 Lateran IV | CCC **1376** | Defines transubstantiation but attributes it to the **Council of Trent (1551), DS 1642** — *confirmed independently at vatican.va*. Not Lateran IV |
| 12 Lateran IV | CCC **1457** | Footnote is "CIC can. 989; Trent DS 1683, 1708" — **no Lateran IV, no DS 812** — and it says nothing about Easter Communion (that is CCC 2042) |
| 13 Lyon I | CCC **2242** | A citizen's **duty to refuse** unjust civil orders — the opposite direction from a Church power to depose an emperor. (2245 fits well) |

| 14 Lyon II | CCC **1031** | Cites Florence and Trent, not Lyon II. (**1032** does cite Lyon II, DS 856) |
| 15 Vienne | CCC **366** | Cites **Lateran V**, not Vienne. (**365** does cite Vienne, DS 902) |

**Roughly half of the citations checked so far do not cite their council.** Of 9 citations across 5
councils, 4 hold up. The shape is consistent: where a council cites two paragraphs, typically one
genuinely cites the council and one is merely topical.

**The pattern:** the paragraphs are chosen because they are *about the same subject* as the council,
not because the Catechism cites that council there. On a site whose value proposition is that its
claims are checkable, a reader who follows `ccc_refs` to confirm "Lateran IV defined
transubstantiation" lands on a paragraph crediting Trent.

This is not necessarily wrong as *topical cross-referencing* — it depends what the field is meant to
do. **That is an editorial decision for the owner**, not a per-entry fix: either (a) keep them as
topical links and label them as such in the UI, or (b) restrict them to paragraphs that genuinely
cite the council. Whichever, the remaining 30 citations should be checked the same way.

Four councils have empty `ccc_refs` (8, 9, 10, 11).

## C3b. Attendance figures are systematically inflated, and trace to one 1913 source

Every council checked so far overstates attendance, and the overstatement has a single origin: the
entries reach for the **1913 Catholic Encyclopedia** figure rather than the critical edition, and
then relabel it. Tanner counts "fathers" — bishops, abbots and religious superiors together — while
the entries print the number as **bishops**.

| Council | Entry says | Sources say |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Nicaea I | "khoảng 318" | The traditional figure, echoing Gen 14:14. Eyewitnesses give 250 / ~270 / ~300; Honigmann reconstructed 194 names |
| 10 Lateran II | ~1000 | Tanner: "at least five hundred" |
| 14 Lyon II | ~500 bishops | Tanner: ~300. The entry's apparent corroborating sources are reprints of the 1913 CE — **one source, not three** |
| 15 Vienne | ~170 bishops | Matches no source. Tanner: 20 cardinals, 4 patriarchs, ~100 archbishops/bishops. 1913 CE: 114 |

This is the "hidden common ancestor" problem the brief warned about, showing up as a measurable
pattern rather than a one-off. The fix is uniform: take the figure from Tanner and label it as
Tanner labels it.

## C4. A repo-wide terminology split — and it runs the other way from what was assumed

`Nixêa` vs `Nicêa` was flagged during the Fathers audit as a cross-cutting split needing a decision.
The councils verifier resolved which side is which: **`content/content.json` (the site's own
Catechism) and all of `content/cong-dong/*` use `Nicêa`. `Nixêa` appears only in the eleven
`content/giao-phu/*` files.**

So the fix belongs in the **Fathers** section, not the councils — the councils already match the
site's Catechism. This corrects the earlier assumption that it was an open repo-wide choice.

## C5. Pope Honorius I appears nowhere in the repository

Constantinople III's own exposition of faith names "Honorius, who was pope of elder Rome" among the
condemned. A grep for `Honori` across the repo returns nothing outside `lateran-ii.json` (a different
Honorius). The condemnation of a pope by an ecumenical council is the single most cited
counter-example to papal infallibility, and a Vietnamese Catholic reader who meets it elsewhere will
find the site silent.

Related, in the same entry: `facts` names only **Agatho** as pope, but he died 10 January 681, eight
months before the council closed, and it was **Leo II** who approved its acts.
